# Visual gesture training and weight versions

The Experiment scene presents two mirrored human hand meshes to the fly's existing compound-eye sensor. The scene menu offers open palms (stand), closed fists (crouch), left-hand pointing (left front leg), right-hand pointing (right front leg), and both hands pointing (both front legs). The other hand remains open for a one-leg cue. Left and right name the human’s anatomical hands. A person facing the fly has their left hand on the fly’s right. Human-left still cues the fly’s left front leg, and human-right cues its right front leg; this is an explicit same-name teaching convention. The model receives retinal signals, never the gesture identifier. The clean lab has no calibration ball or odor-source marker. The hand is visual only and cannot push the fly.

The Brain activity panel is on the right. The Experiment top bar’s two-level inference selector chooses a training lineage and then a weight version. **Load model** starts a paused lab trial with that version's recorded body and neural settings (recorded neural precision, 0.1 ms neural clock, 50 Hz commands; new Apple GPU trials use float16). Selecting a hand starts inference. The existing pause control stops simulation. Loading the base starts an unadapted reference trial.

## Fly response and saved views

The Training page's **View targets** button switches the entire 3D scene to a separate target muscle preview. Its custom **Gesture input** selector covers all five cues and labels the intended action. **Back to result** restores the actual scene and its camera. Experiment has no target-view button. The **Fly response** inset always shows actual results.

The target preview and trainer use the same `routed-muscle-reference-v3` generator. A planning controller constructs a trajectory with support feet on the ground, a 100 ms transition, and the requested lifted feet or lowered body. Planner commands are converted into constant 20 ms muscle commands, matching the neural controller's command rate. A separate free body replays these commands; its measured activations become training labels. No target joint positions are assigned to this replay, and no neural model or weights are modified by the preview. Reference validation is shown for the requested duration and exact body parameters.

The browser remembers the workspace page, training setup and detail tab, target mode and gesture, separate camera views and orbits, scene overlays, brain tab, vision channel and enlarged view, and weight selections. Reloading reattaches to the existing backend without launching training or loading weights automatically. The brain viewer selects neurons in place; only the expand button opens its larger view. Clicking blank canvas clears selection, and the neuron ID lookup shares the scope toolbar.

## Training

Configure iterations, batch size, steps per sample, learning rate, rank (any integer 1–64), seed, and gesture proportions. Choose the original connectome to create a new lineage, or a saved adapter to create a child version. A child inherits its parent's rank, base/body configuration, and adapter parameters; Adam starts with fresh optimizer state. The parent is never edited. Lineage and version names are editable; an omitted version name is derived from the gesture mix and rank.

Each iteration:

1. Generate gesture identities using randomized systematic stratification of the requested relative proportions. A batch's class count is within one sample of its ideal count. Zero-proportion classes are excluded. Across small batches, proportions are matched in expectation.
2. Generate each hand's position and orientation on demand. The hand centers are separated by 200 mm laterally, with palms yawed inward and tilted 50° toward the fly. The shared origin is sampled 100–125 mm ahead, ±6 mm laterally, and 80–92 mm vertically, with ±4°, ±4°, and ±5° rotation jitter. Conservative bounds adjust forward/vertical offsets when needed to keep every cue at least 15 mm ahead and 5 mm above the fly’s local reference plane. This improves retinal separation without changing eye directions, receptor counts, acceptance cones, or contrast.
3. Advance GPU samples together at every neural tick. One graph and adapter are shared; each sample owns its voltages, currents, spike queues, muscle activation, body state, and randomized hand input. Losses are normalized per sample and averaged across the batch. CPU reference execution remains sequential. GPU kernels cover both the sample and neuron dimensions in each dispatch.
4. Average the muscle activation losses and propose one Adam update. Evaluate its actual MSE on a fixed hand placement for each selected gesture, weighted by the configured proportions. Keep every finite update, including those that worsen validation. Invalid numerical updates restore adapter and Adam state and abort the run. Track the best validation adapter separately without changing the ongoing optimizer trajectory.
5. Publish the actual final sample's scene, retinal input, and muscle comparison exactly once. The preview is labeled with the iteration and gesture. It shows the last training sample before that iteration's update. The main curve shows fixed-input validation loss, including the initial baseline. The numeric batch loss and exported sample losses retain the randomized training measurements. Historical rejected updates are marked explicitly, and the zoomed MSE axis prints its range.

Early stopping is optional, with configurable patience (default 10 iterations). When enabled, that many consecutive iterations without meaningful validation improvement stop the run early. Meaningful improvement exceeds max(1e-8, 0.01% of the patience reference loss); smaller improvements still update the best checkpoint. At completion, export the exact best adapter, including the initial adapter if no update beat it. Metadata distinguishes best iteration, saved validation loss, and last validation loss. Iterations are a maximum; metadata records both requested and completed iterations. Validation is used to select weights and is not an independent test of generalization.

Stop requests immediately expose a stopping status, finish the current batch, and pause before the next iteration. Resume retains the same session, Adam state, RNG, history, best checkpoint, and settings in memory. Paused time is excluded from training wall time. A page refresh retains access to the paused run; a backend restart discards it. Start new explicitly discards the paused continuation and uses the setup selections. Completed runs export only adapter factors and metadata.

No images are precomputed for the randomized samples. A seed reproduces the batch identities and transforms under the same implementation. All batch lanes start from independently reset neural and body states. The UI reports the parallel sample count and common step progress. CPU sensing and MuJoCo body updates remain sequential within each command interval; neural forward and backward kernels span the entire batch. Batches exceeding the estimated GPU working-memory budget fail with a request to reduce batch size before allocating their training histories.

## Loss and targets

The targets are measured activation sequences from physically replayed **simulated** muscle commands, not biological recordings. The planning controller is absent from neural training rollouts and inference. Every cue supervises the first **84 main-leg actuator channels**, including the muscles needed to support the body. Other actuators remain outside the loss. The target planner uses only channels with actual mapped motor inputs. For the current full graph, 52 of the 84 channels are routed; the other 32 receive exactly zero target command and activation. The loss still includes all 84 channels. The availability manifest is `backend/flylab/assets/gesture-target-routes.json`, derived from the existing annotation resolver and checked against the source graph. Training obtains its mask from the actual loaded bridge, and only generates references for selected gestures.

- Open palms: stand with all six feet near the floor.
- Closed fists: lower the body by at least 0.20 mm from its reset height.
- Human left hand points: lift the left front foot at least 0.30 mm above the floor.
- Human right hand points: lift the right front foot at least 0.30 mm above the floor.
- Both hands point: lift both front feet at least 0.30 mm above the floor.

The physical check additionally requires uprightness above 0.95, body height above 0.5 mm, and every supporting foot below 0.12 mm. Standing must retain body height within 0.15 mm of reset. These are explicit engineering acceptance tolerances, not biological measurements. Every 20 ms frame from 100 ms onward must pass for the reference to be accepted. Invalid references stop training before optimization; their preview remains labeled unverified. No body parameters are silently changed to make a reference pass.

The generator constrains grounded foot IK to joints with available muscle channels. The two-front-leg goal shifts the reference body 0.1 mm backward to place support under it. It uses planner gains of 200 and 0.2, and the installed force scale. Standing and pointing use mean planner excitation plus 0.075 antagonist coactivation; crouching uses the last planner excitation in each 20 ms interval. Commands stay in [0, 1]. Commands for unmapped channels are zero throughout planning and replay. Beyond 200 ms, the endpoint command is held for standing, crouching, and single-leg poses. The two-leg reference uses a 1.2 mm planning foot-height goal and continues planning from each replay boundary to sustain the lift; no hidden high-frequency body controller acts in the replay. Validation applies to the subsequent 50 Hz replay, not to the faster planner. The extra coactivation and stance channels prevent the unsupported forward settling seen with isolated front-leg commands.

The loss remains mean squared activation error over 84 channels and command times, averaged over the batch. There is no pose, foot-position, or reward term in the differentiable objective. Pose checks qualify demonstration labels; they do not train the neural model directly. The base remains frozen, while gradients may affect any contributing pathway through the shared adapter.

Export actual commands, activation labels, actuator names, and per-frame evidence with `PYTHONPATH=backend uv run python scripts/calibrate_gesture_targets.py --steps 10 --output data/gesture-targets/routed-current-body`. The NPZ and JSON outputs are under `data/gesture-targets/`; replay commands from a fresh body with the exact recorded parameters. The current 196-muscle body uses `peripheral-v2`, `stance-elastic-v1`, and unit mass, strength, and friction. All five routed references pass the complete 100–200 ms check at these settings. Stand and either single-front-leg pose also passed five-second endpoint holds. Crouch and both-front-leg five-second holds did not pass throughout; those longer requests are rejected if selected. Calibration is configuration-specific; earlier baseline mechanics do not automatically pass.

Activation is distinct from force. The differentiable activation update implements the installed unsmoothed MuJoCo muscle activation ODE with the body's Euler activation integration. It is checked against the actual body's activation on every command. Force-length-velocity behavior and rigid-body physics are simulated but are not differentiated. Sensory feedback from physics is treated as an external input at each command boundary.

The demonstrations are engineering targets, not measured biological muscle recordings. Their achieved poses are evaluated and shown as a count out of the selected gestures. Route availability removes one known impossibility but does not prove that shared motor readouts or the constrained adapter can exactly reproduce every reference value. A low activation loss does not establish that the fly is balanced or that the intended pose is reached. A successful activation training run is therefore saved with `behavior_validated: false`.

## Adapter and gradients

The original graph retains every imported neuron, connection, sign, and source count. The effective weights are:

`W_eff[post, pre] = W_base[post, pre] * (1 + 0.75 * tanh(U[group(post)] @ V[group(pre)] / sqrt(rank)))`

New runs use `motor-target-v2` groups: the original `(superclass, rootSide)` partition is refined for motor neurons using recorded `(somaSide, subclass, type)`. This follows the side and muscle annotations used by the existing motor bridge. It separates motor targets that previously shared one VNC group with unknown root side. The current graph has 480 groups; rank 2 trains 1,920 values. Legacy grouping has 46 groups and 184 values at rank 2. Multipliers are positive and bounded between 0.25 and 1.75. This is **a constrained multiplicative low-rank adapter, not standard additive LoRA**. The effective weight difference itself is not necessarily low rank.

Only the factor tensor is an Adam parameter. Original weights do not require gradients. Neural calculations remain in the gradient graph during training. Sparse delivery uses an explicit vector-Jacobian product that considers all existing edges for the input-state derivative, including presynaptic neurons that did not fire. It avoids allocating a dense neuron-by-neuron weight gradient.

Forward execution uses discrete paper-profile LIF thresholds, delays, refractory periods, and every 0.1 ms tick. Backward execution uses a fast-sigmoid surrogate derivative scaled by 0.0003 for new runs (recorded as `surrogate_scale`), with reset and refractory decisions treated as discrete constants. Apple GPU training uses float16 neural state and connection weights, with float32 backward adjoints, loss, adapter parameters, and Adam state. MuJoCo body mechanics retain their existing precision. A custom Metal backward pass retains every connection in its state derivative. Weight-gradient segments are organized by source neuron and group pair, so a zero emitted spike skips only an exactly zero derivative. Batched state gradients share edge-index reads across sample lanes; it does not require MPS sparse autograd. CPU float64 remains the explicit reference and the fallback when MPS is unavailable. Device and precision are recorded in run status, checkpoint settings, and weight-version metadata. Backpropagation spans the complete sample, including voltage, synaptic current, delay queues, firing-rate filters, and muscle activation history. The 20 ms boundaries are recomputation checkpoints, not gradient cutoffs. The default sample is 25 commands (500 ms). Inference uses the existing no-gradient simulator.

## Weight management

The Weights page displays a tree from the frozen original to parent and child adapter versions. Select a node to inspect its settings, gesture mix, parameter count, storage size, final batch loss, initialization, timestamp, and behavioral validation status. Export the run log for per-iteration losses, sample losses, gradient norms, and the last batch's transforms and outcomes.

Each completed run stores a self-contained adapter tensor and metadata in `data/gesture-checkpoints/`. Neither the base graph nor a full brain tensor is duplicated. Optimizer moments are not stored. A child's tensor incorporates its parent's learned adapter, so inference does not need to add a chain of deltas or keep ancestors loaded.

Deletion is branch-based: the UI previews the selected version and every descendant. The backend verifies the exact descendant list before deleting their files and logs. Deletion is refused during training or when the active inference model is in that branch. Earlier SPSA checkpoints remain visible as legacy records that can be inspected and deleted, but cannot seed the gradient trainer.

Checkpoints carry source graph, asset, implementation, dynamics, sensory, group, and rank compatibility information. Forward-model compatibility is fingerprinted separately from training-only code. The audited v4 checkpoint retains its original forward interpretation. A child run can refine a legacy group by copying its parent factor rows to each new subgroup, preserving every effective edge weight at initialization. Incompatible forward models cannot be loaded or continued. Live continuation checkpoints also retain the loaded adapter and hand transform.

## Validation

Focused tests cover exact reference forward dynamics on a deterministic small circuit, nonzero adapter gradients with frozen base weights, sparse backward derivatives against an independent dense calculation, activation integration against MuJoCo, stratification, transform reproducibility, batch publication count, parent/child weight inheritance, deletion protection, cancellation, API validation, and live save/restore.

A short full-graph check used all 166,700 neurons and 25,582,938 edges, rank 2, one pointing sample, five command steps (0.1 simulated seconds), and one Adam update. It completed in about 41 seconds, produced a finite nonzero gradient norm of 0.19664, and changed the 184 adapter parameters by norm 0.07069. This verifies execution and parameter updates, not learned gesture control. That earlier execution check does not validate the current target generator; its physical acceptance check is described above.

The 3D hand assets derive from the MIT-licensed WebXR generic hand mesh. Three.js and MuJoCo use matching baked geometry and world transforms. See `frontend/public/models/hand/LICENSE.md` and `facing-v4/provenance.json` for provenance. The earlier paired meshes remain at their original paths for historical scene rendering. The five paired cues use three fixed finger articulations and do not establish generalization to arbitrary real hands.

The paired-hand scene and additional right/both-leg targets use a new training version. Previous single-hand adapters remain visible in weight history but fail compatibility checks for this task; they are not overwritten or represented as trained bilateral controllers.

## GPU training validation

On September 14, 2026, real Apple GPU tests matched the existing FP16 inference engine exactly for all checked neural state tensors over 20 ms, with zero and 1.8 ms delays. The FP32 backward pass matched an independent dense PyTorch surrogate-gradient calculation. Chunked reductions also cover large and empty adapter groups. These are implementation checks, not equivalence to CPU float64 or learned gesture performance.

A single full-graph comparison used one pointing sample, rank 2, five command steps (0.1 simulated seconds), and one Adam update. GPU FP16 took 12.848 seconds; CPU FP64 took 16.884 seconds (1.31x throughput). Both retained 166,700 neurons and 25,582,938 edges and produced finite nonzero adapter gradients and updates. Initialization and demonstration generation are excluded; reset, sensing, forward/backward execution, body updates, and the optimizer update are included. This is one short comparison, not a sustained or universal speedup. Neural rounding changes trajectories: similar muscle losses do not establish identical dynamics.

Reproduce with `PYTHONPATH=backend uv run python scripts/benchmark_gesture_training.py`. Recorded values are in `docs/results/gesture-fp16-training.json`.

## Parallel GPU batches

GPU batches share the full graph, adapter, and connectivity indexes. Separate sample lanes hold neural and body state, visual input, delayed signals, and backward history. Every 0.1 ms tick advances all lanes; the original sequential implementation remains the CPU reference. The loss is averaged per sample and across the batch, followed by one Adam update. The scene shows the final lane after each iteration, with its own hand placement and measured body response.

Validation covered batch sizes 1, 2, and 3 with zero and 1.8 ms delays, distinct inputs and silence masks, exact neural-state equality to separate execution, averaged gradients, sample export, two-iteration checkpoint creation, and cancellation without saving a partial batch. On one full-graph, three-sample, five-step comparison, sequential GPU execution took 31.48 seconds and parallel execution took 29.29 seconds (1.075x throughput). Peak observed tensor allocation was 1.63 GB, with 2.21 GB allocated by the Metal driver including cached allocations. The largest gradient difference was 1.19e-7, and final joint positions matched exactly. This is one short benchmark using the baseline body and default gains; other workloads, longer training, and larger batches have not been performance-validated. A tested sample-interleaved work ordering was slower and is not enabled.

Reproduce with `PYTHONPATH=backend uv run python scripts/benchmark_gesture_batch.py --batch-size 3 --steps 5`. Timing includes reset, sensing, forward/backward execution, and body updates, and excludes initialization, demonstrations, and the final optimizer update. See `docs/results/gesture-parallel-training.json`.


## September 14 training diagnosis

The reported ten-iteration run changed its adapter but improved randomized batch MSE by only about 0.16%. Its old teacher commanded 32 channels without any motor route, creating an unavoidable MSE contribution of about 0.00857. The new routed references remove that structural target error while still producing the requested poses in an independent physics replay. This changes the reference dataset: absolute v1 and v2 losses must not be compared as learning progress.

A controlled backward-kernel comparison kept the old partition, inputs, targets, and all 166,700 neurons / 25,582,938 edges identical. Five steps with one sample decreased from 11.77 to 8.67 seconds; three samples decreased from 30.77 to 17.82 seconds (1.73x). Loss values were identical and relative gradient differences were below 1.4e-7. These timings exclude the new validation guard and do not claim the same whole-run speedup. See `results/gesture-training-performance-fix.json`.

A batch step advances every sample; it is expected to take longer than a step for one sample. Compare seconds per sample or samples per second. The optimizer's export separates training and validation wall time. Kernel optimizations, corrected targets, motor grouping, and validation safeguards do not change base topology, forward neural precision, or the neural tick count.

Reproduce the isolated learning audit with `PYTHONPATH=backend uv run python scripts/verify_gesture_learning.py --settings-from data/gesture-checkpoints/<version>.pt --iterations 5 --output docs/results/gesture-learning-fix.json`. The source checkpoint supplies settings only, and the audit starts from the original connectome. All generated adapters are temporary. `results/gesture-learning-fix.json` records the fixed validation baseline, attempted and accepted updates, actual batch losses, route diagnostics, and measured timing. Improving validation activation MSE does not establish learned gesture-controlled poses.

The final routed-target audit used five updates, batch size three, ten 20 ms commands per sample, rank two, and the reported run's body, sensory, and gain settings. Fixed validation MSE decreased from 0.01243198 to 0.01230092 (1.05%). Every iteration accepted an improving full or reduced Adam proposal. The unrouted-target MSE contribution was exactly zero. This is modest activation learning over fixed validation inputs, not demonstrated gesture-controlled leg poses.


### GPU submission concurrency

Live workbench access and gesture training share an execution lock. Training releases it at command boundaries and while paused; initialization, neural commands, device copies, optimizer validation, and checkpoint tensor export use the same lock. This prevents simultaneous Metal command-buffer synchronization and encoding from separate Python threads. Samples within a batch remain parallel GPU lanes. The change follows a September 14 native Metal assertion (`setCurrentCommandEncoder`, SIGABRT) with one thread in MPS synchronization and another in a GPU-to-CPU copy. Saved models retain their forward compatibility.


## Full-sample gradient computation

GPU training stores exact FP16 neural boundary states and replays each 20 ms block with the unchanged forward kernels during its reverse pass. Scratch tapes are reused, and the initial forward pass records only the boundary state rather than writing a 200-tick tape that will be discarded. All neurons, edges and 0.1 ms ticks remain present. Checkpointing trades extra forward computation for bounded per-block tape memory; it does not make a longer sample as cheap as a shorter one.

Long surrogate-gradient products can exceed FP32 range. The reverse pass carries one shared binary exponent for state adjoints and accumulated adapter-factor derivatives, aligning each local muscle loss before accumulation. This is numerical rescaling, not clipping at checkpoint boundaries. The existing unit global-gradient norm limit applies once after the complete sample/batch gradient is formed. Very large unclipped norms are reported through their base-10 logarithm. Relative contributions below FP32 resolution can still be lost, as with ordinary floating-point arithmetic.

This differentiates the neural recurrence and muscle activation dynamics. Discrete reset/refractory decisions retain the existing surrogate conventions; ray sensing and MuJoCo kinematics remain detached. It is not exact differentiation of hard spikes or the entire physical environment. The earlier short-window benchmark results above describe the earlier implementation.

The default duration gives visual signals more opportunity to reach motor neurons: the diagnostic configuration first showed a visual effect there around 360 ms. That is a measured configuration-specific latency, not a guaranteed response time. Full-history gradients and a longer trial do not themselves demonstrate successful gesture learning.

Validation of the full-history implementation: dense PyTorch surrogate checks cover loss credit crossing command boundaries with zero and 1.8 ms delays; scaled and unscaled gradients agree on the small reference, and parallel gradients agree with independent samples. Validation without tapes reproduces forward neural states exactly. The full graph completed three parallel 500 ms samples in about 85 s for forward plus backward (excluding validation/optimizer), with finite globally clipped gradients. See [full-history measurements](results/gesture-full-history.json). With the original surrogate scale of 1, the unclipped norm was approximately 10^187, so numerical scaling prevents overflow but does not resolve the underlying exploding surrogate-gradient behavior. Successful pose learning remains unproven.

[Scratch reuse measurements](results/gesture-full-history-buffers.json) found identical loss and clipped gradients, with reused-buffer times of 17.1–17.9 s versus allocation times of 17.6–23.0 s for three 100 ms samples. Timing variance is too large to establish a reliable steady-state speedup. Memory reuse, native-precision checkpoints, and omission of unused validation tapes reduce overhead without changing the forward equations; they do not remove the cost of a longer history.

The GPU training preview is published immediately after the batch forward pass, before backward computation and validation. It shows the last sample under the pre-update weights. Backward checkpoint progress and elapsed time update independently; validation continues selecting the best weights without delaying that preview.

The backward derivative has since been calibrated without changing forward dynamics. See [surrogate calibration](SURROGATE_CALIBRATION.md) for the seven-candidate comparison, selected scale, repeat-update results, and remaining limits.
